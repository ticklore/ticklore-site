Executive Summary

The thesis in brief

Ticklore is a ticketing platform built around a simple idea: a ticket shouldn't be thrown away after the event — it should become a keepsake.

Where existing NFT ticketing platforms compete on fraud prevention and resale control, Ticklore leads with sentimental and collectible value. That positioning fits a far wider range of events than concerts alone: family reunions, benefit fundraisers, community gatherings, corporate events, and milestone celebrations.

The platform hides blockchain complexity from the end user entirely. Attendees buy with a credit card and receive a ticket by email — no wallet setup, no crypto knowledge, no technical friction. Every ticket is a one-of-one digital record the owner keeps permanently, with the option to move it into a personal wallet as self-custody becomes more mainstream.

Critically, the ticket is not the end of the product — it is the key. Each ticket unlocks a permanent, private vault of the event's memories: photographs, written recollections, the record of who was there. Ticketing acquires the customer; the vault is what they keep, and what they come back for.

Ticklore is designed from day one as a general-purpose platform, not a single-vertical tool. The go-to-market plan is to prove the model with one real, well-chosen first event, then expand deliberately across event categories. We are raising a pre-seed round to fund initial development and our first live pilot event.

The Market Opportunity

An underserved wedge, not the whole market

Industry research places the global online event ticketing market in the range of roughly $60–90 billion in 2026, growing at a mid-single-digit to low-double-digit CAGR depending on the source. Ticklore is not targeting this entire market — it is targeting an underserved wedge within it.

Large platforms (Ticketmaster, AXS, Eventbrite) are built for scale — major venues, national tours, high-volume events — and are not designed around, or particularly attentive to, small nonprofits, family gatherings, or community organizers.

Existing NFT ticketing platforms (GET Protocol, YellowHeart, SeatlabNFT) target concerts and festivals specifically, competing on fraud prevention and resale royalties — a pitch with limited resonance outside live music.

Ticklore's wedge is the large, fragmented long tail of smaller organizers — reunions, galas, community events, corporate gatherings — underserved by both categories above, combined with a keepsake-first positioning no competitor currently owns.

Note for investors The figures above are drawn from third-party market research and presented as directional context, not a bottoms-up projection of Ticklore's addressable revenue. A detailed TAM/SAM/SOM model specific to our target segments should be built as part of due diligence.

Why Now

  • NFT infrastructure — minting, wallet-abstraction tooling, low-fee networks — has matured enough that a small team can ship a production-grade product without deep blockchain R&D.
  • Consumer wallet adoption is trending upward. Building optional self-custody in now positions Ticklore ahead of that curve rather than retrofitting for it later.
  • No incumbent — large or NFT-native — currently owns the "keepsake" positioning for non-concert events, leaving a clear, defensible wedge open.

The Problem

  • Paper and PDF tickets are disposable. Once the night is over, no lasting record of the event exists.
  • Ticket fraud and duplication remain persistent problems for organizers of all sizes, especially smaller nonprofits and community groups without access to enterprise tooling.
  • Nonprofits and community organizers are underserved by major platforms built for scale, not for a 200-person gala or a family reunion.
  • Donors increasingly want transparency into where their money goes. Most nonprofit ticketing tools provide no verifiable, permanent record of fund allocation.
  • Existing NFT ticketing platforms lead with fraud prevention or resale royalties — a value proposition that resonates with concert promoters and means little to a family planning a reunion.

The Solution

Three principles

Ticklore is a ticketing platform where every ticket minted is a unique digital record the buyer keeps permanently, positioned first and foremost as a keepsake rather than a financial instrument.

Invisible blockchain

Buyers pay with a card and receive their ticket by email. No wallet setup, no crypto purchase, no technical friction. The technology should be as unremarkable to the user as the payment network behind a credit card swipe.

One of one

Every ticket is a unique record — event, date, seat or role, price paid, and where relevant the donation it carried — permanently tied to its holder.

Transparent by design

For fundraising events, the platform can show in aggregate how ticket-linked donations were used — a trust feature most nonprofit tools cannot offer.

The Living Ticket

Before and after the event

The core differentiator in Ticklore's roadmap is a hybrid data model that lets a ticket's story keep growing after the event ends, without ever compromising the integrity of the original record.

Before — the locked original

The moment a ticket is minted, its core facts are written permanently and can never be altered or deleted. This is the ticket's Chapter One, and it is the foundation the rest of the product depends on.

After — the growing addendum

Once the event has happened, the organizer can add new content: photographs, an attendee roster, highlights, a closing note. These additions are append-only — new entries can be added, but nothing already recorded can be overwritten or removed. Each addition is timestamped, so the history of what was added and when becomes part of the permanent record itself.

Why this matters

This turns each ticket into a genuine, growing keepsake rather than a static receipt: a family reunion ticket that shows the whole family's photographs months later, or a fundraiser ticket that shows the outcome the donation helped fund. No existing NFT ticketing platform offers this; all of them treat the ticket as finished the moment the event ends.

A practical note Rich content such as photographs and detailed rosters is stored off-chain with only a permanent reference recorded on-chain — standard practice, and what keeps the model both affordable and genuinely tamper-proof. Sensitive attendance data, for example a private support-group or wellness event roster, remains private or opt-in only, never public by default.

The Keepsake Vault

Where the durable business is

The ticket is the key. The vault is what it opens.

Every event gets a permanent, private archive — photographs, written memories, the record of who was there — unlocked by proving ownership of a ticket. The blockchain holds the tamper-proof fingerprints and the access rules; the content itself lives in permanent off-chain storage.

Ticketing is a fee race to the bottom against companies far larger than us. Memories have no competitor.

This is the strategic centre of the business, and it is worth stating plainly why. Ticket fees are a commodity: every competitor can undercut them, and the giants can afford to. But an organizer who has placed four hundred family photographs into a permanent archive does not casually switch platforms next year. The vault is where pricing power and retention actually live. Acquire with the ticket; retain and monetize with the vault.

How it is priced — and the line we will not cross

  • Pay once, never a subscription. A recurring fee on a keepsake creates a hostage: pay or lose your memories. That is brand suicide for a product built on sentiment, and we will not build it.
  • Permanent by default for photographs and written memories, funded up front from the event's base price — an endowment model, where part of the setup fee pays for genuinely permanent storage.
  • Video and heavy storage are a paid upgrade, priced to pass real cost plus a fair margin.
  • The organizer controls contributions — a switch between locked (organizer only) and open (attendees contribute, subject to organizer approval). Approval matters when the record is permanent.
  • Charge the organizer, never the attendee. Money enters through the organizer's front door, up front, as a business decision. A paywall in front of a grandmother's photographs poisons the exact feeling the product exists to sell.

Ownership & Transferability

What the holder actually owns

A keepsake the holder cannot control is not a keepsake. Ticklore's position is that the holder owns the ticket outright — with rules that protect the event itself while it is happening.

Locked before and during the event

Tickets cannot be transferred in the run-up to or during an event. This removes the scalping and duplication problems at the source, without any need for a resale marketplace or enforcement apparatus.

Unlocked afterward

After a window set by the organizer — with a platform minimum of thirty days — the ticket unlocks. From that point the holder may keep it, gift it, pass it down, or sell it. It is theirs.

What travels, and what stays

If a ticket is sold, Chapter One — the permanent, verifiable event record — travels with it. That is the part with genuine provenance value. Post-event addendum content, such as family photographs and attendee rosters, is licensed to the holder at the time of publication and does not transfer with a resale. A stranger buying a reunion ticket receives the artifact, not the family album. That is the correct outcome in both directions.

No platform royalty

Ticklore takes no cut of secondary sales. This is a deliberate stance rather than an omission. A resale royalty would put the company's revenue on the same side as ticket price appreciation — directly contradicting our commitment never to position tickets as investments. We would rather have a consistent story than a marginal revenue line.

Custody Wallets are created silently for buyers through third-party wallet-as-a-service infrastructure rather than key management we build ourselves. An optional self-custody export path is designed in from the start, so ownership claims remain credible to crypto-native observers.

Business Model

Revenue streams

  • Per-ticket platform fee — a small percentage or flat fee applied at checkout. The primary model is direct-to-attendee sales; organizers may also purchase a batch outright.
  • Tiered organizer plans — free or low-cost for small community events; paid tiers for higher-volume organizers needing custom branding, analytics, or donation-tracking dashboards.
  • Keepsake vault setup and upgrades — a one-time, up-front charge to the organizer covering permanent storage, with video and heavy media as a priced upgrade. Never a subscription.

Cost structure

  • Smart contract development and network fees, minimized by building on Base, a low-fee Ethereum layer-2 network.
  • Permanent storage funded up front per event, sized so the endowment covers the obligation.
  • Platform hosting, payment processing, and customer support.
  • Early stage: primarily founder time plus the raise outlined below.

Technical Approach

What is built, and what it deliberately does not do

Two systems, kept strictly separate. Stripe is the cash register; the smart contract is the deed office. Money never touches the contract, and the contract never touches money.

This is not a technicality. A contract that holds funds is a contract that can be drained, and it is the single largest source of catastrophic failure in this industry. Ticklore's contract holds no balance, has no withdrawal function, and has nothing worth stealing. Payment is handled by ordinary payment infrastructure that businesses have used for years; the contract only ever records facts and enforces rules.

Current state

  • Network: Base, an Ethereum layer-2 chosen for consumer-grade tooling and low fees.
  • The core ticket contract is written and tested — ERC-721, with per-ticket event name, date, tier, price paid, donation amount, check-in status, and transfer rules.
  • Ticket artwork is generated entirely on-chain. The card image is not hosted on any server; the contract draws it from scratch on request. If Ticklore disappears, the tickets still render.
  • Door check-in is a single owner-only operation that marks a ticket used and cannot be repeated.
  • 17 automated tests passing, covering minting, access control, transfer locks, permanent non-transferability for sensitive events, and metadata integrity.

Before any real money moves

Three things are deliberately scheduled ahead of a live paid event, and are steps rather than oversights: a third-party security audit; an emergency pause capability; and separate staff roles, so door staff can scan tickets without holding the master key.

Appendix A

How Ticklore Works

A plain-language guide to the technology behind the platform — written for organizers, partners, and anyone who wants to understand what is actually happening under the hood.

What is a blockchain?

A blockchain is a shared record book.

Imagine a ledger that thousands of computers around the world each keep an identical copy of. When something new is written in it, every copy updates at once. Anyone can read it, and once an entry is written it cannot be erased or altered — not by us, not by anyone. Changing a past entry would require simultaneously rewriting thousands of independent copies, which is why the record holds.

That permanence is the entire reason Ticklore uses one. A paper ticket can be lost, faked, or thrown out. A record in a company's private database survives only as long as the company does. An entry on a blockchain is durable in a way neither of those is.

What this means for Ticklore When a ticket is created, the essential facts — the event, the date, the seat or role, who it was issued to — are written into a permanent public record. Ten years from now that entry still exists and still says the same thing, whether or not Ticklore is still here.

What is an NFT?

An NFT is a one-of-a-kind entry in that record book.

The distinction is between things that are interchangeable and things that are not. Any dollar bill is as good as any other dollar bill — swap them and nothing has changed. But the deed to a specific house is not interchangeable with the deed to a different house. It refers to one particular thing.

"NFT" stands for non-fungible token, which is a technical way of saying: not interchangeable. One of one.

The term carries baggage from a period when NFTs were mostly traded as speculative art. That association is unfortunate, because the underlying technology is far more mundane, and far more useful, than that era suggested. At its core it is a way to establish that a specific digital item belongs to a specific person — the digital equivalent of a titled, registered object.

What this means for Ticklore Every ticket is its own distinct record. Not a copy, not a duplicate, not one of a batch of identical PDFs.

What is a wallet?

A wallet is the thing that proves a record belongs to you.

It is less like a physical wallet and more like a signature that cannot be forged. It does not hold your ticket the way a billfold holds cash; the ticket lives on the blockchain. The wallet holds the proof that the ticket is yours.

Setting one up is the single largest point of friction in consumer blockchain products, and it is where most people give up. So Ticklore does not ask anyone to.

What this means for Ticklore A wallet is created silently for each buyer at checkout by specialized third-party infrastructure. Attendees never see it, never set it up, and never have to understand it. For those who want direct control, an export path to a personal wallet is available.

What is a smart contract?

A smart contract is a set of rules that enforces itself.

An ordinary contract is a document describing what should happen, relying on people and courts to make it so. A smart contract is a program living on the blockchain that simply does what it says, automatically, the same way every time, for everyone.

The value is that the rules are not promises. They are not policies a company can quietly revise. They are written into the record itself.

What this means for Ticklore "This ticket cannot be used twice." "This ticket cannot be transferred until thirty days after the event." "This ticket can never be transferred at all." These are not terms of service. They are written into the contract itself. We could not break them for you, and we could not break them against you.

On-chain and off-chain

Not everything belongs in a permanent public record. Storing a hundred family photographs directly on a blockchain would be prohibitively expensive, and would make private moments permanently public.

So the model is split. The proof lives on-chain; the content lives off-chain.

The comparison is a property deed. The deed is filed at the county courthouse — permanent, public, verifiable by anyone. The photo album from the day you moved in is at your house. The deed proves the house is yours. The album is what you actually look at.

What this means for Ticklore The ticket's core record is on-chain, permanent and verifiable. Photographs, rosters, and post-event content are stored separately, with a permanent reference on-chain proving they are authentic and unaltered. This keeps costs low, keeps private content private, and still guarantees nothing has been tampered with.

Putting it together

Here is what happens when someone buys a Ticklore ticket, in order:

  • They pay with a card. Standard checkout. Nothing unfamiliar.
  • A wallet is created for them silently, managed by specialized infrastructure. They are never asked to set anything up.
  • A smart contract creates their ticket — a one-of-one record written permanently to the Base network, with the event details and rules built in.
  • The ticket arrives by email. Viewable, scannable at the door, theirs to keep.
  • The ticket is checked in at the event. The contract marks it used. It cannot be used again.
  • After the event, the story continues. The organizer adds photographs, a roster, a closing note. Each addition is timestamped and permanent. Nothing already written can be removed.
  • The ticket unlocks. After the organizer's chosen window, the holder can keep it, gift it, pass it down, or sell it. It is theirs.
At no point in that sequence is the buyer required to understand any of the above. That is the design goal.

The technology should be as invisible as the payment network behind a credit card swipe — present, essential, and entirely unremarkable to the person using it.
